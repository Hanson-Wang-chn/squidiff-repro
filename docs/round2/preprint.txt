                                         Abstract
     Single-cell sequencing has revolutionized our understanding of cellular hetero-
     geneity and responses to environmental stimuli. However, mapping transcrip-
     tomic changes across diverse cell types in response to various stimuli and
     elucidating underlying disease mechanisms remains challenging. Studies involv-
     ing physical stimuli, such as radiotherapy, or chemical stimuli, like drug testing,
     demand labor-intensive experimentation, often hindering the rapid advancement
     of mechanistic insight and drug discovery. To address this, we present Squidiff,
     a diffusion model-based generative framework designed to predict transcriptomic
     changes across diverse cell types in response to a wide range of environmental
     changes. We demonstrate Squidiff’s robustness across various scenarios, includ-
     ing cell differentiation, gene perturbation, and drug response prediction. Through
     continuous denoising and semantic feature integration, Squidiff effectively learns
     transient cell states and predicts high-resolution transcriptomic landscapes over
     time and conditions. Furthermore, we applied Squidiff to model the develop-
     ment of blood vessel organoids and cellular responses to neutron irradiation and
     growth factors. Our results demonstrate that Squidiff enables in silico screening of
     cell molecular landscapes, facilitating rapid hypothesis generation and providing
     valuable insights for precision medicine.

     Keywords: Transcriptomic prediction, diffusion model, gene perturbation, cell
     differentiation, drug testing, blood vessel organoids, radiotherapy




Introduction
Cells in biological systems coordinate and respond to environmental changes col-
lectively, with responses varying by location and cell type due to both intrinsic
tissue heterogeneity and external signaling cues[1, 2]. These living cells are com-
plex, dissipative systems that operate far from chemical equilibrium and often exhibit
highly non-linear responses[3]. While single-cell sequencing has enabled the unbi-
ased characterization of cellular heterogeneity, predicting transcriptomic changes in
responses to stimuli remains challenging. Exploring disease mechanisms and optimiz-
ing drug components require sequencing large-scale screens, which is both laborious
and expensive[4, 5].
    Several machine-learning based models, such as scGEN[6], CellOT[7], GEARS[8],
and GenePert[9] have been developed to predict single-cell perturbation using
variational autoencoders (VAE)[10], optimal transport methods[7], graph neural
networks[8] and large language models. Despite the advances they offer, these models
face limitations in predicting high-resolution dynamic transcriptional responses, espe-
cially in highly transient states such as in organ development. Reconstructing smooth
transitions of learned features remains challenging. Multiple cell types or cross-cell
type transfer of prediction is also not considered in these methods. Additionally, these
models require both the unperturbed transcriptomic and perturbed data as inputs but
do not fully leverage underlying biological knowledge to guide their predictions, often
leading to failure in interpolating cell states.


                                              2
    Diffusion models have demonstrated remarkable potential in data generation,
capable of producing high-quality and realistic samples through iterative refinement
processes and learning richer and more complex data distributions than varia-
tional autoencoders[11–13]. Their integration with variational autoencoders further
enhances their ability to model smooth feature transitions and capture complex latent
spaces[14]. Recent perspectives highlight their potential in creating virtual cells estab-
lishing them as a promising approach in artificial intelligence[15]. However, their
application in predicting single-cell perturbations or cell differentiation remains largely
untapped. Here we introduce Squidiff (Single-cell QUantitative Inference of stimuli
responses by DIFFusion models), a computational framework designed to predict tran-
scriptomic responses of diverse cell types to a spectrum of environmental changes,
including cell differentiation, gene perturbation, and drug treatment. Squidiff is a con-
ditional denoising diffusion implicit model[16] which generates new transcriptomes
that represent distinct cellular states. Squidiff excels in predicting the differentiation
of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) into mesendoderm and endoderm, guided by
stimuli vectors. Notably, Squidiff captures transient cellular states that other methods
often miss. Moreover, it effectively predicts non-additive gene perturbation and cell
type-specific drug responses, as demonstrated in its application to analysis of temporal
cell states in glioblastoma dataset in response to novel drug combinations.
    We applied Squidiff to investigate neovascularization and vasculopathy induction
in blood vessel organoids exposed to high linear energy transfer (LET) radiation,
uncovering potential mechanisms related to radiation-induced changes in vascular dif-
ferentiation trajectories. Organoid technologies, in this case blood vessel organoids
derived from human iPSCs, have shown promise in disease modeling and as drug test-
ing platforms, especially in the context of self-organizing models that can start from a
single source of stem cells[17]. This platform is thus particularly useful for understand-
ing injury and identifying potential drug candidates with targeting extreme injury
states on human cells, such as high LET radiation exposures. In particular, these mod-
els are critical for scenarios that cannot be feasibly tested with real patients, including
the risks anticipated in deep space missions to the Moon and Mars. Despite the ben-
efits of 3D systems for modeling cell types in vitro, the complexity of organoids and
the labor-intensive nature of drug testing have limited the widespread application of
these models.
    To evaluate Squidiff’s capabilities in the characterization of cellular dynamics
and facilitating candidate drug discovery, we constructed blood vessel organoids and
probed the potential mechanisms of neutron radiation damage, associated with sec-
ondary radiation sources present in deep space. Using Squidiff, we predicted the
molecular profiles of various cell compositions within blood vessel organoids through-
out their differentiation and development, including endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and
mural cells – the principal components of blood vessels. We validated these predictions
with real experimental single-cell sequencing. Interestingly, Squidiff suggested a poten-
tial developmental pathway from mural cells to endothelial cells, consistent with recent
observations in a time-series study of blood vessel organoids[18]. It also predicted the
effects of irradiation on various cell types by generating the single-cell transcriptomic

data upon irradiation, even when only limited data on irradiated cell types were avail-
able, and identified the affected signaling pathways at each time point of injury. By
incorporating an FDA-approved radioprotective drug, G-CSF, Squidiff predicted that
G-CSF might have a protective effect by promoting vascular specification compared
to irradiated groups. These findings provide a detailed assessment of cell heterogene-
ity and differentiation trajectories in vessel organoids exposed to radiation and G-CSF
proteins. Squidiff thus shows the power of characterizing high-resolution cell dynamics
under environmental changes by leveraging the strengths of diffusion models.

Results
Squidiff predicts transcriptomic changes through denoising
Squidiff is a diffusion model designed to predict single-cell transcriptomic changes in
response to cell state changes such as cell differentiation, development, and exposure
to various physical or chemical stimuli across different cell types (Fig. 1A). Leveraging
a neural network architecture and continuous denoising processes, Squidiff can predict
both future and past cell states when stimuli are specified, and can further predict the
effects of multiple drug combinations as well as multiple gene perturbations.
    Squidiff integrates a diffusion model for data generation with a variational autoen-
coder (VAE) for encoding latent representations[10, 12, 18]. This integration enables
the generation of new transcriptomic data while modulating cellular states and condi-
tions using latent variables (Fig. 1B). Specifically, the VAE serves as an encoder that
maps cell data as well as treatments into a unified latent representation space compris-
ing semantic variables (zsem ), while the diffusion model can generate any target cell
conditioned on the semantic variable via a standard denoising process (see Methods
for details). Overall, Squidiff is capable of generating transcriptomic data reflecting
cell-type variations, cell-state transitions, and cell-type-specific responses to multiple
stimuli such as drug and gene perturbations.
    To explain the Squidiff framework, we first show an application to synthetic data
generated using Splatter[19]. We simulated single-cell transcriptomics data for three
cell types, introducing noise at each step to visualize the diffusion processes (Supple-
mentary Fig. 1). The semantic latent variable (zsem ) captured meaningful variations
in gene expression associated with specific cell states or responses to stimuli, resulting
in a clear separation between different conditions in the latent space(Fig. 1C). Addi-
tionally, Squidiff can encode input data into a stochastic subcode xT by running its
deterministic generative process backwards. However, xT primarily captures stochas-
tic details rather than retaining the semantic information of the input (Fig. 1C), as
the diffusion process introduces noise to the input data. The reverse diffusion process
then denoises and reconstructs the data given the condition, successfully recovering
the original transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 1D), demonstrating the model’s ability to
accurately capture and reproduce cellular states.
    To generate new single-cell gene expression data over time and in response to
stimuli, Squidiff employs two methods of latent manipulation: interpolation and addi-
tion (Fig. 1E). Addition involves combining the original latent representation with

Squidiff predicts cell differentiation
Squidiff demonstrates the ability to predict transcriptomic profiles of specific cell
states when the transcriptome of a starting point and the latent embedding learned
by Squidiff for the applied stimuli are known. This capability is particularly useful
for investigating the evolution of cells or missing intermediate states in cellular differ-
entiation and development. The approach leverages the generative capabilities of the
diffusion model and its manipulation of semantic latent variables.
    Applied to a public single-cell transcriptomics dataset of iPSC differentiation as an
example[20], Squidiff effectively captures the state changes from iPSCs to mesendo-
derm and definitive endoderm cells over a period from day 0 to day 3 (Supplementary
Fig. 2). The model was trained on a random split of data at day 0 and day 3, extract-

For instance, NANOG emerged as a pluripotency marker at day 0[21], with its expres-

a progressive increase. By applying pseudotime analysis to the combined input data

latent variable embeddings from the ground-truth data of day 2 and day 3 do not
exactly match the interpolated variables, likely due to the different growth factors
applied each day in this experiments. This suggests that zsem represents an averaged
trajectory.

Squidiff predicts gene and drug perturbation
Another application of Squidiff is predicting cell responses to gene perturbations or
drug treatments. Unlike scGen, which requires training to acquire the latent repre-
sentation of given conditions, Squidiff can generalize a trained model across different
conditions. For example, given a model trained exclusively on single-cell RNA tran-
scriptomics perturbed by two genes, scGen cannot acquire the latent representation of
control data without additional training. However, Squidiff can extract embedding and
use the learned representation of individual gene perturbation to successfully predict
the transcriptomic change of wild-type cells upon these perturbation (Supplementary
Fig. 3).
    Furthermore, given the semantic and stochastic latent variables for two-gene per-
turbations, Squidiff assumes that two-gene perturbations are represented as the sum of
two latent variables (Fig. 3A). Genes interact synergistically to produce non-additive
genetic effects upon multiple gene perturbations, which cannot be accurately modeled
by additive approached. We tested Squidiff on an example involving non-additive per-
turbation using single-cell RNA transcriptomics of K562 cells perturbed by ZBTB25
and PTPN12, as discussed by GEARS[8]. Despite not using graph knowledge as a
model prior, as GEARS[8], Squidiff achieved highly accurate predictions (Fig. 3B).
Comparative analysis with GEARS and scGen revealed that Squidiff consistently pro-
duced more robust results (Fig. 3C-D). Thus, Squidiff exhibits significant capability
in predicting gene perturbations and elucidating underlying mechanisms.
    Besides gene perturbation, we further tested Squidiff’s performance by applying it
to drug screening (Fig. 3E). Unlike gene perturbations, where the affected gene lists are
known, drug effects are often complex, involving influences on multiple genes concur-
rently and the regulation of associated pathways. Thus, a model capable of predicting
the overall cellular response to drugs is valuable for exploring drug mechanisms and
identifying the most effective drugs with minimal side effects. A study involving drug
tests on glioblastoma highlighted the distinct effects of Etopside and Panobinostat on
tumor cells. We trained the Squidiff model on randomly selected myeloid cells with six
drugs, untreated tumor cells, and oligodendrocytes (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. 3).
The model showed strikingly accurate predictions of the six drug effects on tumor cells
and oligodendrocytes, demonstrating strong performance in predicting transcriptomic
changes upon drug treatment (Fig. 3G). Squidiff also identified Panobinostat as hav-
ing the strongest effect on tumor cells compared to other cell types among all screened

drugs, consistent with the finding from the reference (Fig. 3H)[27]. To further vali-
date the drug effects on tumor cells, we plotted UMAP embedding of cells with and
without treatment and observed the distinct effects of Etoposide and Panobinostat as
previously reported (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Squidiff predicts cell differentiation in blood vessel organoids
To evaluate Squidiff’s unique capability in predicting the cascade of transcriptional
programs during continuous cell state transitions, such as development and how they
are altered with stimuli, we employed organoid technology as our experimental plat-
form. Organoids, differentiated from human pluripotent stem cells, have emerged as
a novel 3D biomimetic system for modeling human tissue development[28]. Over the
last decade, organoids have been extensively used as disease models and drug test-
ing platforms, including brain, liver, and lung organoids, among others[29]. However,
as self-organized systems, organoids are inherently heterogeneous, and the interac-
tions among cells within them are complex[29]. Current single-cell and spatial RNA
sequencing techniques have been applied to unravel this cellular heterogeneity and
profile gene expression patterns during their differentiation trajectory. Despite these
advances, it remains challenging to fully characterize and quantify the entire develop-
mental processes of organoids at any given time point. While pseudotime analysis of
single-cell RNA sequencing provides snapshots of organoids development. at specific
time points, real-time gene expression profiling across different organoids is challenging
due to the labor-intensive workflows and technical expertise requirements for manip-
ulation of these engineered models[29, 30], in addition to the costly sequencing steps.
Here, Squidiff addresses these limitations by predicting cell transcriptomic changes
during development and in response to stimuli.
     Taking Blood vessel organoids (BVOs) as a case study for applying Squidiff, these
organoids were differentiated from human iPSCs and directed to develop into endothe-
lial and vascular lineages, enabling a vascular network-like structure to form as early as
Day 6 (Supplementary Fig. 5). BVOs have emerged as a novel biomimetic system for
modeling human vascular development and disease and serve as a powerful platform
for drug discovery[18].
     The fate and state transition of cells in blood vessel organoids have been studied
in real-time[18], providing a ground truth for comparison. In these studies, progeni-
tor states in early BVOs were observed to bifurcate into endothelial and mural fates,
highlighting the potential of BVO mural cells to differentiate into endothelial lineages
(Fig. 4A). To test whether Squidiff could replicate these critical transitions, we gen-
erated BVOs from healthy human iPSCs to create a training dataset. We fixed the
organoids, dissociated them into single cells, and performed single-cell RNA sequenc-
ing on day 11. The BVOs were exposed to differentiation processes from day -1, and
promoted to mesoderm aggregates from day 3 to day 5, with vascular formation start-
ing on day 6. As a result, we identified fibroblasts, mural cells, endothelial cells, and
progenitor cells in the organoids on day 11 (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. 5A).

Structure damage and metabolic alterations in blood vessel
organoids induced by neutron irradiation
Squidiff demonstrated the ability to predict the cellular responses to stimuli, including
gene perturbations and drug perturbations above. We next evaluated whether Squidiff
could predict physical perturbations such as irradiation, on different cell types. Ioniz-
ing radiation, whether from radiotherapy, nuclear-reactor accidents, or in anticipated
deep space travel, presents a significant risk to human health[31]. Radiation expo-
sures impact not only cancerous but also non-cancerous cells, particularly those that
are rapidly proliferating, such as endothelial cells, blood and immune cells, and local
parenchymal cells within the radiated area[32]. High-energy particles, such as neu-
trons, can penetrate biological tissues, causing damage at the cellular and molecular
levels[33]. Understanding the effects of radiation on human tissues can help develop
effective countermeasures and treatments for radiation-induced damage[34]. Degener-
ative vascular changes have been identified as one of the main radiation health risks
to astronaut crews on exploration missions[35]. Microvascular endothelial cells are
susceptible to ionizing radiation, and radiation-induced alterations in endothelial cell
function are thought to be critical factors in organ damage through endothelial cell
activation, enhanced leukocyte-endothelial cell interactions, increased barrier perme-
ability, and initiation of apoptotic pathways[32, 36]. Blood vessel organoids, which
mimic the human vascular system, provide an excellent model for studying these
effects.
    During the BVO development stage on day 5, we exposed the cultures to either
neutrons or protons radiation and continued the BVO culture until day 11 (Fig. 5A).

BVOs and those exposed to neutron irradiation. The single-cell RNA sequencing
revealed the presence of endothelial cells, mural cells, and fibroblasts. However, the

alterations in the molecular landscape (Fig. 5B). Squidiff effectively learned radia-
tion exposure as a vector in the semantic space, enabling the generation of perturbed
transcriptomes for different cell states in response to radiation.
    To validate Squidiff’s capability in data prediction and biological exploration, we
masked the mural cells and fibroblasts entirely during Squidiff training, considering
only the endothelial cells under irradiation and control conditions (Fig. 5B). The model
successfully generated the transcriptomic data under irradiation with high accuracy
(Fig. 5C). We then applied the model to the predicted data interpolated from the
healthy sample to acquire the predicted irradiated data for endothelial cells, mural
cells, and fibroblasts. The UMAP of all the data indicated these cells at the early devel-
opment stage might be the most affected stage (Fig. 5D), likely due to the immaturity
of cell types and the lack of tissue structure and cell interactions. The ranking of the
most differentiated genes upon irradiation across cells indicated continuous changes
along the course of BVO developmental days (Supplementary Fig. 7).
    Intriguingly, genes behaved differently over days and across cell types. For instance,
the importance of MYCT1 in endothelial cells decreased in the middle period and
resumed at the late stage of development, while MYCT1 remained one of the most
differentiated genes in mural cells and fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 7E). The most
affected genes upon irradiation across all these cells included upregulation of genes
such as CDKN1A, MDM2, GDF15, ACTA2, ABCA1, MMP14, and ITGA6, and
downregulation of genes such as NNAT, HIST1H1D, TOP2A, HMGB2, and PRC1
(Fig. 5E). These genes are involved in cellular responses to ionizing radiation, cell
motility, DNA damage through P53 pathways, apoptosis, TNF-α signaling via NF-
kB, KRAS pathways, protein kinase B signaling, and inflammation responses in gene
enrichment analysis (Supplementary Fig. 8).
    Notably, CDKN1A is a key gene implicated in radiation response, known for its role
in cell cycle regulation and DNA damage repair. MDM2 is another significant gene,
functioning as a negative regulator of the p53 tumor suppressor, thus playing a criti-
cal role in controlling cell proliferation and apoptosis[37]. Using a live-dead assay, the
percentage of dead cell was much higher in irradiated BVOs (Supplementary Fig. 9),
consistent with the apoptosis process triggered by the radiation effects. The size of
BVOs immediately after neutron irradiation and before the vascular induction showed
a decreased size (Supplementary Fig. 9A). We found that the culture supernatant
post-irradiated was enriched in IL1-beta and TNF-alpha, indicating enhanced induc-
tion of inflammation (Fig. 5F), consistent with the increased expression of GDF15,
a stress response gene associated with the regulation of inflammation and cell sur-
vival. These metabolic changes overall resulted in downstream failures in vascular
formation and abnormal distribution patterns in BVOs (Fig. 5G), causing earlier
vascular sprouting in the radiation BVOs as compared to healthy controls. Overall,
Squidiff effectively generated data with biological relevance and predicted the complex
molecular responses to radiation exposure.

Treatment potentials of G-CSF against radiation disruption in
blood vessel organoids
In recognition of the disruption of vascular structures, the apoptosis of cells triggered
by the p53 pathway, and protein kinase B signaling, we attempted to understand
whether the FDA-approved radioprotective drug, granulocyte colony-stimulating
factor (G-CSF), could rescue the defects in vasculogenesis in vitro. G-CSF is a gly-
coprotein that plays a crucial role in the production, differentiation, and function of
granulocytes, a type of white blood cell essential for fighting infections[38]. It has been
used in clinical settings to stimulate the bone marrow to produce more white blood
cells, particularly after chemotherapy or bone marrow transplants. Recent studies have
suggested that G-CSF may also have protective effects on vascular tissues, promoting
endothelial cell survival and enhancing tissue repair processes[39]. A known effect of
G-CSF is its activation of MEK1/2, PI3K/Akt and NF-κB pathways (Fig. 6A)[40],
consistent with findings on the regulation of NF-κB and kinase B signaling on radia-
tion injury (Supplementary Fig. 8). However, studies on G-CSF effect on fibroblasts
and mural cells in the vasculature remain limited.
    Squidiff offers a way to predict G-CSF effect on other cell types when only one
cell type (e.g. endothelial) responses are known. We sequenced the single-cell RNA
expression in GCSF-treated BVOs and considered it as ground truth data for the
model as a case.
    Given the training data on endothelial cells only (Fig. 6B), Squidiff successfully
generated single-cell transcriptomics for fibroblast and mural cells (Fig. 6C, Supple-
mentary Fig. 10). To further validate the biological relevance of these gene expression
predictions, we conducted differential gene expression analysis comparing real irradi-
ated data with predicted G-CSF-treated data. Notably, we identified distinct sets of
differentially expressed (DE) genes for each cell type, with some genes shared across
multiple types (Supplementary Fig. 10B-C). The biological processes identified for
enriched genes revealed unique roles for each cell type in response to G-CSF treat-
ment. Fibroblasts were primarily associated with processes involved in blood vessel
morphogenesis and vasculogenesis, indicating their critical role in structural develop-
ment. Endothelial cells showed enrichment in pathways regulating apoptosis and the
cell cycle, highlighting their involvement in maintaining cell survival and prolifera-
tion. Mural cells, in contrast, were linked to pathways related to genomic stability, cell
cycle progression, and mitosis, indicating their role in maintaining genomic integrity.
These findings aligned with the observed reduction in cell death percentages in G-CSF-
treated samples compared to those affected by radiation (Supplementary Fig. 9B).
This study demonstrates Squidiff’s capability to predict cell type-specific responses to
drug treatments and provides valuable insights into the effects of G-CSF on irradiated
blood vessel systems.

predict transcriptomic changes across diverse cell types in response to a spectrum of
environmental changes. Our findings demonstrate that Squidiff provides a robust and

changes in cell states over varying conditions and time points through the generation

therapeutic responses, positioning itself as a valuable tool in precision medicine.
    Compared to existing generative models such as scGen, variational autoencoders
(VAEs), Squidiff surpasses these approaches in its ability to model complex cell-state
transitions and predict responses to gene and drug perturbations with high temporal
resolution. While previous models like scGen rely on latent variable interpolation, their
generative processes are often less effective in capturing smooth state transitions, par-
ticularly for rare or transient states. In contrast, Squidiff leverages diffusion processes
to generate high-fidelity predictions of intermediate and transient cell states, which
are crucial for understanding differentiation trajectories and the effects of environmen-
tal perturbations. Furthermore, our study highlights Squidiff’s capacity to integrate
conditional factors, enabling precise control over conditions and making it adaptable
across various biological scenarios.
    We applied Squidiff to blood vessel organoids to investigate the impact of neu-
tron irradiation and subsequent G-CSF treatment, a radioprotective intervention. The
human vascular system plays a central role in maintaining blood flow, nutrient dis-
tribution, and metabolic balance across all organs, making vascular health critical for
understanding long-term risks in deep space missions. Exposure to cosmic radiation is
anticipated to disrupt the sensitive endothelium, leading to structural damage, altered
cell phenotypes, and diverse pro-inflammatory or pro-regenerative responses. Previ-
ous studies have already suggested the risk of vascular malformations and excessive
angiogenesis under these conditions, with implications for cancer and other diseases
[41–43].
    In our work, we utilized advanced in vitro 3D models consisting of iPSC-derived
organoids alongside Squidiff’s predictive capabilities to elucidate the temporal dynam-
ics of cellular responses. One key advantage of Squidiff is its ability to predict and
validate cell type-specific responses using a single timepoint dataset, thus circum-
venting the high costs and sample heterogeneity challenges of repeated single-cell
sequencing across multiple timepoints.
    Using human BVOs as a model system, Squidiff accurately identified radiation-
induced damage to endothelial cells and elucidated the mechanisms underlying G-CSF
treatment in promoting endothelial cell maturation over stromal cell differentia-
tion post-radiation injury. Specifically, we found that BVOs subjected to neutron
irradiation exhibited significant shifts in differentiation trajectories, leading to an
increased generation of mural cell populations that disrupted vascular formation
during organoid maturation. Despite single-cell RNA sequencing being performed
one week post-irradiation, Squidiff successfully reconstructed the precise cell-specific
changes occurring during the initial acute phase of injury. This capability allowed us
to identify temporal gene responses that could serve as promising drug targets for
radioprotection.

    Moreover, our analysis revealed the potential therapeutic effects of G-CSF proteins
on vascular tissues, providing insights into enhancing G-CSF delivery efficacy and ther-
apeutic index in vivo. The discovery of these radiation countermeasures opens avenues
for future studies exploring G-CSF and other protective agents in space medicine and
related fields.
    Despite the promising results, there are several limitations in the application of dif-
fusion models like Squidiff. The training process requires introducing Gaussian noise
into the data, which leads to prolonged training times. Additionally, the computational
resource requirements for diffusion models are higher compared to other generative
frameworks such as VAEs or GANs. These challenges underscore the need for opti-
mizing training protocols and exploring more efficient implementations of diffusion
models.
    In the future, improving the scalability and speed of Squidiff will be essential
to broadening its application to large-scale single-cell datasets. Additionally, while
Squidiff proved effective in identifying cell-state transitions and responses to specific
perturbations, further validation using in vivo models and more diverse datasets would
strengthen its applicability to clinical settings. Integrating Squidiff with multi-modal
omics data, such as proteomics and epigenomics, could also enhance its predictive
power and uncover novel regulatory mechanisms driving cell fate decisions.

Methods
Diffusion probabilistic models (DPMs) have proven to be powerful methods for gen-
erating new data in various domains, such as images[12], videos[44], and protein
structures[45] in biomedicine. These models have also shown high potential in single-
cell reconstruction and inference[13], although their actual implementation in genetics
is still limited. This limitation may be due to the unique challenges of applying DPMs
to generate gene expression data, particularly the need for semantic manipulation
and meaningful interpretation of the generated expression profiles. Recent advances
in combining DPMs and variational autoencoders (VAEs) have demonstrated effec-
tive representation learning in the image domain[14]. Building on these advancements,
we adapted the diffusion autoencoder model, also a conditional denoising diffusion
implicit model (DDIM) model for single-cell gene expression prediction under various
perturbations. The goal is to learn a semantically rich latent space that allows smooth
interpolation while maintaining the reconstruction capability that the diffusion model
excels at.

Similar to other diffusion autoencoder models, the goal of Squidiff is to model the
target distribution of perturbed gene expression by learning a denoising process at
varying noise levels. The diffusion process and reverse process are the two major
components of the squiff model.

Semantic encoder
The goal of the semantic encoder is to provide zsem for the reverse process to generate
transcriptomics conditioned on zsem . The semantic encoder is designed to capture
high-level semantic features from the input data, which are then used to condition the
reverse diffusion process, ensuring that the generated transcriptomics data adheres to
the desired semantic characteristics.
    Our semantic encoder is implemented as a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with con-
ditional batch normalization layers and a residual connection for embedding time and
semantic features. The architecture consists of two linear layers with ReLU activations,
each followed by a conditional batch normalization layer. The conditional batch nor-
malization layers adaptively normalize the input features based on the batch statistics,
which is particularly useful when the batch size is small or when there is variability
in the input data distribution.
    In addition, a residual connection is incorporated to embed both the time and
semantic features, enhancing the model’s ability to capture temporal dynamics and
complex semantic relationships in the data. This residual connection helps maintain
the integrity of the original input features while allowing the model to learn more
abstract representations.
    The semantic encoder is trained as part of the overall model. During training,
the semantic encoder takes the input data and produces zsem , which is then used in
the reverse diffusion process to conditionally generate the transcriptomics data. The
training process ensures that the generated data is semantically coherent and adheres
to the desired characteristics specified by zsem . zsem = Enc(x0 ).

Sinusoidal position embeddings
To incorporate temporal information, we use sinusoidal position embeddings. These
embeddings use sine and cosine functions of varying frequencies to encode positional
information, providing scale invariance, smoothness, and a wide range of frequencies.
This method is efficient and parameter-free, ensuring that the positional information
is effectively captured without additional learned parameters.

Processing of single-cell RNA sequencing data for model
training
In the section on the prediction of iPSC differentiation, we first log normalized the gene
counts and selected the top 500 variable genes for the task considering the computing
capabilities. The training data are split into 60%, 30%, and 10% for the train set, test
set, and validation set, respectively, which turns out to be 4800 cells for training when
the data on day 1 and day 2 were not masked out, 2400 cells for testing, and 800 cells for
validation. We then add Gaussian noise to the training data to determine the number
of steps in the diffusion process (Figure S2). The results indicate the optimal number
of diffusion steps. The whole model is trained on one H100 with 80 GB memory.

Reprogramming and culture of human pluripotent stem cells
(iPSCs)
We cultivate the wild-type iPSC line (WTC) using mTeSR plus medium on growth
factor-reduced Matrigel-coated 6-well plates. The cells are usually passaged every 3-4
days based on the proliferation rate.

Generation of blood vessel organoids
To model the vascular malformations seen in PS, we generated blood vessel organoids
using previously established protocol[37, 46]. The embryoid body (EB) was first formed
in Aggrewell® from iPSCs obtained one day prior to the induction of differentia-
tion (Day -1). On day 0, CHIR and BMP4 were added to induce the differentiation
of embryoids into the mesoderm germ layer. On day 3, vascular differentiation
was induced in the organoids by applying VEGFA and forskolin. The differentiated
organoids were then embedded in Matrigel® on day 5 and cultured in medium con-
taining VEGFA, FGF2, and FBS. The organoids were fixed on day 11 and stained for
light sheet microscopic imaging[47]. We stained for endothelial and mural cell markers
(CD31+, PDGFRβ+, αSMA+, respectively) to confirm the generation of a vascular
network constructed by endothelial tubes and mural cells, with visualization of the
spatial arrangement of these cells in the vasculature.




                                                21
Radiation exposure
On Day 5 of BVO generation, we performed radiation exposure with either 1 Gy
neutrons or 4 Gy photons and the BVOs continued to be cultured until day 11. Neu-
tron irradiations were performed at the Columbia University Radiological Research
Accelerator Facility (RARAF), using an accelerator-based neutron irradiator[48], as
described previously[49]. The system was designed to mimic the neutron energy spec-
trum from an Improvised Nuclear Device[48, 50], but has also been used to model
the high linear energy transfer components of galactic cosmic radiation[51]. Dosimetry
was performed on the day of irradiation, as previously described, using a custom-built
Tissue Equivalent Proportional Counter (TEPC) which measured the total dose, and
a compensated Geiger–Mueller dosimeter, which had a very low response to neutrons
and thus measures only the photon component. Photon irradiations were performed at
Columbia University’s Center for Radiological Research using a Gammacell 40 137Cs
irradiator (Atomic Energy of Canada Ltd) with a dose rate of 0.68 Gy/min for varying
low-energy doses (i.e., 4 Gy).

Drug treatment
Following radiation exposure, the medium from the blood vessel organoids was col-
lected and replaced with fresh vessel organoid medium. GCSF at a concentration of
80 ng/mL was then administered to the samples immediately.

Cytokine testing
Culture medium was collected on days 0, 3, and 6 post-radiation to assess cytokine
levels. The concentrations of TNF-α and IL-1β in the irradiated blood vessel organoid
(BVO) medium were measured using ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The wash
buffer was prepared by diluting the concentrated solution with distilled water accord-
ing to the instructions provided with the ELISA kits. Standard cytokine solutions
were prepared by serially diluting the provided standards with the assay diluent to
generate a standard curve. Irradiated BVO medium samples were added to each well
of the 96-well microtiter plates. The plates were covered and incubated for 2 hours
at room temperature. Following incubation, the detection antibody was added, and
subsequent steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The
absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a plate reader.

g of BSA, 250 µL of Triton X-100, 250 µL of Tween 20, and 47.5 mL of PBS in 50
mL buffer) and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C. The samples were
then washed with PBS-T solution and embedded in mounting medium.

Single-cell sequencing
Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed in BVOs on day 11. Blood vessel organoids
were dissociated into single cells using enzymatic digestion with a dissociation buffer
containing trypsin or collagenase. The dissociated cells were counted using an auto-
mated cell counter, and cell viability was assessed using trypan blue exclusion.
Single-cell suspensions were then loaded into a microfluidic device, such as the 10x
Genomics Chromium Controller, to capture individual cells in nanoliter-scale droplets
containing reagents for cell lysis and reverse transcription. Unique molecular iden-
tifiers (UMIs) and cell barcodes were incorporated to enable accurate identification
and quantification of transcripts from individual cells. After reverse transcription, the
cDNA was amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and purified. Sequencing
libraries were constructed from the amplified cDNA using a standard library prepa-
ration kit and indexed with unique sample barcodes for multiplexing. The libraries
were sequenced on a high-throughput platform Illumina NovaSeq, to generate paired-
end reads optimized for comprehensive coverage and accurate quantification of gene
expression.
     The raw sequencing data were processed using the Cell Ranger pipeline (ver-
sion 6.1.2) to demultiplex reads, collapse duplicates based on UMIs, align reads to
the GRCh38 reference transcriptome, and quantify gene expression levels for each
cell. Quality control steps removed low-quality cells and potential doublets, and the
high-quality single-cell RNA-seq data were analyzed for normalization, clustering, dif-
ferential expression, and trajectory inference to elucidate the transcriptomic changes
in response to different treatments.

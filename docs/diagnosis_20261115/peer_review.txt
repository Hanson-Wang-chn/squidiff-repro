Reviewers' Comments: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors present a very interesting approach combining a diffusion model with a variational autoencoder to predict single-
cell transcriptomic trajectories during development, as well as perturbations in response to drug exposure and radiation. The
show high accuracy of their model relative to two other tools with similar goals: scGen and GEARS. The paper is very well-
written, with exciting results, well-described figures, and detailed Methods. I do not have any major alteration
recommendations, mostly minor editorial comments. 
1. On page 2, it is not fair to say that “Multiple cell types or cross-cell type transfer of prediction” is not addressed by existing
tools. This is the goal of scGen, as well as scVIDR [PMID: 37602218]. 
2. On page 4, xT needs to be better explained. What does a “stochastic subcode” mean? This is not a commonly used term or
concept. 
3. Related to the above point, the Figure 1 title states “a semantic latent space (zsem), and a diffusion process to generate xT.”
Like the description of Zsem, xT should also be described in a few words here, with maybe a reference to “See text for details.” 
4. Also in Figure 1 caption: … “(A, B, C, D1, and D2), ” panels D1 and D2 are not marked on the figure itself. 
5. On page 6, paragraph 1, “Linear Interpolation (Lerp)”: is Lerp a term from scGen? Is it necessary to use the abbreviation?
There is also a reference to “Lerp providing a direct path …”. The term is best dropped unless essential. “Linear Interpolation”

PAGE 4
should suffice. 
6. Page 8, “approaches” is misspelt: “… which cannot be accurately modeled by additive approached”. 
7. Page 8: “A study involving drug tests on glioblastoma highlighted the distinct effects of Etopside.” – Etoposide is misspelt. 
8. Figure 2, Panel B: is the UMAP visualization from original gene expression space or latent space? 
9. Figure 2: the boxplots in Panel E were not clear to me. What is the Y-axis? Also, the colors of the lines in the Cell Density vs.
Pseudotime plot need to be explained in a legend. 
10. Figure 2, panel F: Likewise, what do the red, yellow, blue circles denote? Days? If so, the progression seems to be from
right to left. This is counterintuitive and should be reversed to left to right. 
11. Figure 3, panel E: What do the looped vectors on top left denote? Needs to be explained. 
12. Page 15, paragraph 1: References to NFkB are not showing the character “k”. 
13. Page 15, paragraph 3: Figure 6D is never referred to, 
Overall, this is a very impressive and important article, and I would like to commend the authors on their excellent work.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
In this manuscript, the authors introduced Squidiff, a diffusion model-based method that uses transcriptomic data as training
sets to develop a generative model. This method is capable of predicting gene expression distributions under perturbation
including cell differentiation, gene and drug perturbation, irradiation and radiation. The authors mainly compared the
performance of Squidiff with scGen, a VAE-based method, on gene and drug perturbation and cell differentiation data. They
especially claimed that Squidiff could reconstruct smooth transitions of cells by a denoising process which variational
autoencoder-based models could not do. 
In all, the novelty of this study is represented by employing a diffusion model-based deep-learning approach, which better
leverages information from environmental variables than previous models. Besides, I appreciate the authors' effort in applying
and validating their method using datasets from various biological scenarios. Especially, the authors designed an experiment
in validating Squidiff’s ability in predicting the effect physical perturbations using BVO cells. Finally, the manuscript is well-
organized and not hard to follow. 
While predicting the transitions of cells responding to perturbations is interesting, there are several concerns, regarding the
method description, validation, and explanation of the results. The authors need to provide more details on the data
preprocessing and model training. In addition, the method requires state-of-art GPUs and is time consuming in running time,
and this may affect broad application of the method. 
Major issues: 
1. An important application of Squidiff lies in predicting the impact of environmental changes on transcriptomics. However, the
authors do not provide a clear explanation of how these environmental changes are quantitatively defined or how they are
integrated with transcriptomic data during the training process. Specifically, it is unclear whether the characteristics of
environmental changes are directly used as input variables or indirectly incorporated through latent representations (e.g.,
z_sem). A more detailed explanation of this aspect of the modeling process would be beneficial. 
2. In the Reverse Process subsection, the definition of 
ϵ
_θ is unclear. Are the definitions of 
ϵ
_θ (x_t,t) and 
ϵ
_θ (x_t,t,z_sem )
the same? The authors need to review the definition of p_θ (x_(t-1)│x_t,z_sem )=N(f_θ (x_1,1,z_sem,0)) to ensure its
accuracy. Additionally, transcriptomics data does not obey a Gaussian distribution, and the authors need to explain whether
this has an impact on model performance. 
3. In Figure 3A, the two-gene perturbations are represented as the linear interpolation of two latent variables. This is
interesting, but the authors need more validations to support the assumption. Why is interpolation performed in the latent space
rather than in the gene expression space? What properties of the latent space justify this choice? This is not clear from the
semantic encoder. And I am interested in its ability to predict the effects of drug combinations on cell transitions, can this
method be trained with single drug perturbations and then used to predict the effects of multiple drugs? 
4. In Figure 3D, the results of CTSL, SAT1 and TIMP4 seem not very good. Is there any reason for selecting these genes?

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
Overview: 
The authors introduce Squidiff (Single-cell QUantitative Inference of stimuli responses by DIFFusion models), a diffusion-
based framework for predicting cellular responses to perturbations over time. Squidiff is evaluated across diverse and impactful
tasks, including cell differentiation, gene and drug perturbations, radiation-induced structural damage, and treatment effects in
organoids. Notably, the method demonstrates statistically significant improvements over established models such as scGen
and GEARS in predicting expression levels in unseen data. 
Beyond its benchmarking success, Squidiff is validated against blood vessel organoid (BVO) data, showcasing its ability to
model relevant biological processes even when key time points or cell types are held out during training. The model’s
capability to generalize and predict complex cell development trajectories under varied stimuli is a major advance for the field.
Squidiff represents an innovative and comprehensive framework for perturbation response prediction and cellular development
modeling. 
Strengths: 
- Many experiments showcasing Squidiff’s capabilities. 
- Validated on new data, showing its ability to capture relevant biological function. 
- Superior performance on benchmarks against commonly-used methods such as GEARS and scGen. 
- Uses a diffusion architecture, which is not necessarily new (e.g. scDiffusion, scDiff), however seems to be the first to be
thoroughly evaluated on cell development trajectories with strong results 
Overall comments: 
There seem to be some typos and grammatical mistakes throughout the manuscript. A few are listed below: 
- Formatting error: page numbers overlap with figure captions 
- P. 9 in the first paragraph under the section “Squidiff predicts cell differentiation in blood vessel organoids”, there is an
unnecessary period in “...snapshots of organoids development. at specific…” 
- P. 12 in the first half, there is an extra space after the parenthetical reference to Fig. 4H, and awkward pluralization in “mural
progenitors features”: “...RNA data[18] (Fig. 4H) . The existence of differentiating EC cells from day 5 to day 9 shown as mural
progenitors features…” 
- P. 19 fix the grammar here “...allowing the encode of x0 to latent code xT…”

- P. 19 need a space after the period “...on an extra latent variable z_sem.The objective is…” 
- P. 19 formatting errors on the equations at the bottom of the page 
- P. 20 one of those versions of z_sem should be chosen at the end of the section “Semantic encoder” 
- P. 21 in “Stochastic encoder” the first sentence has awkward grammar “...process enables the encode of input gene
expression…” 
- P. 21 missing period after the definition of x_t+1 in “Stochastic encoder” 
- Authors may consider citing the following relevant works: 
scDiffusion (Luo et al., 2024) – single cell diffusion model 
scDiff (Tang et al., 2023) – another single cell diffusion model 
Questions: 
- The VAE is mentioned as an encoder for the latent representations, but the semantic encoder in the Methods section does not
describe a VAE architecture. It only explains that it is a standard MLP encoder. Furthermore, the loss function in the Training
section does not contain a KL divergence term, which would be necessary for a VAE. Is there a VAE anywhere in this
architecture? If not, it seems several edits are in order. 
If I have misunderstood and there is a VAE somewhere in this architecture, what is the motivation for using the DDIM+VAE
architecture over a standard conditional DDPM or DDIM? An ablation showing the value of the VAE would be helpful, for
example, on the synthetic data in Figure 1. 
- What is the reason for selecting the 203 genes as opposed to keeping all 500 highly variable genes in Figure 3C? 
- The MMD and Correlation values are extremely close to perfect. Have you looked at correlations between deltas of control vs
perturbations or top n differentially expressed genes (e.g. as is done in the GEARS or scGPT papers)? Models are typically
less likely to saturate on these metrics, and they would provide a clearer picture of the difference between the methods. 
- This is not quite clear to me on page 14: “The model successfully generated the transcriptomic data under irradiation with
high accuracy (Fig. 5C). We then applied the model to the predicted data interpolated from the healthy sample to acquire the
predicted irradiated data for endothelial cells, mural cells, and fibroblasts.” Can you clarify what is being predicted? Are you
first predicting the held out cell types on day 11, and then generating the rest of the trajectory using the latent delta vector
between healthy and irradiated cells? 
- Can you clarify how the model is able to generate meaningful latent embeddings on unseen cell types in the irradiation
section? This would be an unusually strong kind of generalization, but perhaps I am misunderstanding how the expression is
being generated in this case.

Editorial suggestions:  
We also suggest adding some benchmarking data comparing Squidiff to existing similar methods, eg PRNet 
published in Nature Communications. 
 
Response: We appreciate the editor’s insightful suggestions on benchmarking PRNet (Qi et al, 2024) with 
Squidiff. PRNet is a perturbation-conditioned deep generative model designed to predict transcriptional 
responses to novel chemical perturbations. It uses a Perturb-adapter to encode drug compounds and dosages 
within a variational autoencoder (VAE) framework. In contrast, the original Squidiff model relies solely on single-
cell transcriptomics data as input and does not account for drug information. To benchmark effectively, we have 
updated Squidiff with an option that also enables the encoding of drug information. Specifically, we adopted the 
drug-rescaled Functional Class Fingerprints (rFCFP) from PRNet, which updates the Squidiff semantic latent 
variable 𝑧!"# to 𝑧!"#′=𝐸𝑛𝑐(𝑥$,𝑟𝐹𝐶𝐹𝑃) (Fig. R1A, Fig. 3H). With this enhancement, we benchmarked the 
performance of Squidiff and PRNet using the sci-plex3 dataset (Srivatsan, S. R. et al. 2020), specifically 
evaluating their ability to generalize to unseen drug components (Fig. R1B, Fig. 3I). We also tested the 
performance under a random split of the data (Fig. R1C, Fig. 3I). Our results show that Squidiff performs 
comparably to, or in some cases surpasses, PRnet.     
 
Figure R1. A. Schematic diagram of Squidiff integrated with drug-rescaled Functional Class Fingerprints 
(rFCFP). B-C. Performance evaluation of Squidiff and PRNet using Pearson correlation and R-squared scores 
in out-of-distribution perturbation scenarios, including both unseen compound split and random split in the sci-

Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comments. The goal of Squidiff is to generate gene 
expression profiles under various conditions. Performing linear interpolation in gene expression space is not 
feasible due to the high dimensionality, complexity, and inherent experimental noise, including batch effects and 
non-linear dependencies on perturbations.  Instead, Squidiff leverages a latent space learned by the semantic 
encoder, which captures biologically meaningful features of a cell’s state in a structured and smooth manner,

preserving essential biological variations. Moreover, the latent space is designed to allow perturbation effects to 
be additive. Linear interpolations correspond to meaningful cellular transitions rather than arbitrary mathematical 
mixtures of gene expression vectors. Additionally, the latent space inherently encodes cellular states along with 
properties such as perturbation awareness, cell type identity and developmental stage. This structured 
representation enables the model to capture key transcriptomic patterns while filtering out spurious variations, 
justifying why interpolation in the latent space is preferable.  
Regarding Squidiff’s ability to predict the effects of drug combinations on cell state transitions, its 
architecture is inherently suited for generalization. The assumption is that the latent space captures additive or 
synergistic interactions implicitly. Due to the limited availability of single-cell RNA sequencing data related to

molecular and morphology properties of melanoma cells treated with various drug compounds using a novel

initial validation of Squidiff's ability to generalize to drug perturbations (Fig. R2). However, we acknowledge that 
the limited cellular features in this dataset may constrain Squidiff's ability to fully capture complex drug 
interactions compared to transcriptomic data.

3. In Figure 3A, the two-gene perturbations are represented as the linear interpolation of two latent variables. 
This is interesting, but the authors need more validations to support the assumption. Why is interpolation 
performed in the latent space rather than in the gene expression space? What properties of the latent space 
justify this choice? This is not clear from the semantic encoder. And I am interested in its ability to predict the 
effects of drug combinations on cell transitions, can this method be trained with single drug perturbations and 
then used to predict the effects of multiple drugs? 
 
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comments. The goal of Squidiff is to generate gene 
expression profiles under various conditions. Performing linear interpolation in gene expression space is not 
feasible due to the high dimensionality, complexity, and inherent experimental noise, including batch effects and 
non-linear dependencies on perturbations.  Instead, Squidiff leverages a latent space learned by the semantic 
encoder, which captures biologically meaningful features of a cell’s state in a structured and smooth manner, 
which is a similar strategy as scGen (Lotfollahi et al 2019). Operating in this latent space reduces noise while 
preserving essential biological variations. Moreover, the latent space is designed to allow perturbation effects to 
be additive. Linear interpolations correspond to meaningful cellular transitions rather than arbitrary mathematical 
mixtures of gene expression vectors. Additionally, the latent space inherently encodes cellular states along with 
properties such as perturbation awareness, cell type identity and developmental stage. This structured 
representation enables the model to capture key transcriptomic patterns while filtering out spurious variations, 
justifying why interpolation in the latent space is preferable.  
Regarding Squidiff’s ability to predict the effects of drug combinations on cell state transitions, its 
architecture is inherently suited for generalization. The assumption is that the latent space captures additive or 
synergistic interactions implicitly. Due to the limited availability of single-cell RNA sequencing data related to 
drug combinations, we evaluated Squidiff on an alternative dataset 4i (Bunne, C. et al. 2023), which profiles 
molecular and morphology properties of melanoma cells treated with various drug compounds using a novel 
microscopic technology.  
To assess Squidiff’s predictive capability, we withheld drug combination data during training and trained 
Squidiff using only control cells and cells treated with single drugs, including trametinib, panobinostat, dabrafenib, 
erlotinib, and midostaurin. We then used Squidiff to generate single-cell data for drug combination treatments 
and compared the Pearson correlation between predicted and ground-truth responses from the 4i dataset. While 
4i does not contain single-cell transcriptomic data and provides only ~50 features per condition, it serves as an 
initial validation of Squidiff's ability to generalize to drug perturbations (Fig. R2). However, we acknowledge that 
the limited cellular features in this dataset may constrain Squidiff's ability to fully capture complex drug 
interactions compared to transcriptomic data.
